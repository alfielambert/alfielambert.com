Job postings are one of the cleanest ways to see where a company is actually investing. Strategy pages tell you what a company wants the market to believe. Hiring tells you which capabilities it's prepared to spend money building.

That distinction matters more than it sounds. A company can put "AI-first" on its homepage for the cost of an afternoon. Opening five machine learning requisitions is a materially different signal — it shows up in budget, in headcount planning, and usually months before the resulting product ever gets announced.

## Reading hiring data as a signal

Most market research leans on things companies say publicly: press releases, product launches, conference talks. All of that is true, but all of it is also curated. Job postings are closer to a company's internal reality, for a simple reason: nobody writes a requisition to impress a journalist.

That makes hiring data useful for a few different questions:

- Which sectors are increasing investment in a given capability
- Which specific technologies are appearing more often in job descriptions
- Where a company is quietly building a team around something it hasn't announced
- Where hiring demand is slowing, which is often a leading indicator before it shows up anywhere else

### A worked example

Take a hypothetical mid-size SaaS company. Its public messaging is steady quarter over quarter — nothing in the changelog suggests a shift in direction. But its job board tells a different story: three postings for "LLM evaluation," two for "applied ML," and a new "AI platform" team appearing in a senior hire's title.

None of that is proof of a specific product. It is, however, a much stronger prior than anything on the company's homepage.

In an illustrative dataset like this, you'd typically walk the analysis in this order:

1. Pull postings for the sector or company list you care about
2. Classify each posting against a fixed set of capability keywords
3. Aggregate by month to see the trend, not just a snapshot
4. Cross-reference against headcount or funding data, if available
5. Treat the result as a prior to investigate, not a conclusion to publish

<InBrief>
- Job postings reflect budget and headcount decisions, not messaging
- They move earlier than press coverage, product launches or investor updates
- Structured well, they turn into a repeatable market-intelligence signal rather than a one-off research exercise
</InBrief>

## Why this is harder than it sounds

The obvious objection: a single job posting is noisy. A company might be backfilling, over-hiring speculatively, or using inflated titles for recruiting purposes. All fair. The signal isn't in any one posting — it's in the trend across many, filtered against a consistent taxonomy.

> A company can say a capability is a priority. Opening five roles for it, and keeping them open, is a much harder signal to fake.

That's really the whole argument. Individual data points are weak. Structured, aggregated and tracked over time, they become one of the more honest signals available about what a company is actually doing.

Here's a simplified illustration of what a monthly rollup might look like:

| Month | ML-related postings | Sector average | Signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jan | 3 | 1.2 | Above average |
| Feb | 4 | 1.4 | Above average |
| Mar | 2 | 1.1 | Above average |
| Apr | 1 | 1.3 | In line |

Small tables like this are easy to skim and easy to keep updating without redesigning anything.

## A minimal classification pass

For technical readers, a first-pass classifier is usually just keyword matching against a controlled vocabulary before anything more sophisticated is worth building:

```python
KEYWORDS = ["machine learning", "llm", "applied ai", "ml engineer"]

def classify(posting_text: str) -> bool:
    text = posting_text.lower()
    return any(keyword in text for keyword in KEYWORDS)
```

That's deliberately unglamorous. Most of the value in this kind of analysis comes from consistent classification and a clean pipeline, not from a clever model on day one.

## Where this fits alongside other signals

Hiring data isn't a replacement for talking to customers, reading filings or tracking product changes directly. It's a complement — evidence that's cheap to collect, updates continuously, and tends to move before a company's public narrative catches up with what it's actually doing.

For more on the kind of systems this connects to, see the [hiring signal intelligence](/#building) project, or read more about [Schema.org's JobPosting type](https://schema.org/JobPosting) if you want the formal structure most job boards already publish against.

The rest is mostly discipline: a consistent taxonomy, a repeatable pipeline, and treating any single data point as a prior rather than a conclusion.
